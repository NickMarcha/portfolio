I’m doing a master’s degree in informatics at the University of Bergen, specializing in visualization. The programme mixes computer graphics, GPU programming, algorithms and a lot of reading research papers, and the introductory visualization course sits at the center of it. That course is where the rest started to make sense to me. Graphics, data handling, statistics and user studies all meet in visualization, at the point where a person is trying to understand something.

This article is my attempt to explain the field to anyone. It starts with things you already know, like weather maps and bar charts. It works its way up to the three examples the programme itself uses to describe the field: how a hospital scanner turns a stack of X-ray slices into a 3D image of a skull, how engineers draw the invisible flow of air around a car, and how you show a huge hierarchy like the file system on your computer.

1. What visualization is

The short version. A visualization is a picture made from data so that a person can do something with it. Check the weather, spot a problem, compare options, convince a friend. If the picture doesn’t help someone do something better than they could without it, it is decoration.

A working definition

The definition most people in the field use comes from Tamara Munzner, whose textbook was the backbone of the course:

Computer-based visualization systems provide visual representations of datasets designed to help people carry out tasks more effectively. [Munzner 2014]

Every part of that sentence matters. “Datasets” means the picture is driven by data, not drawn by hand. “People” means there is a human looking at it. “Tasks” means that person is trying to do something specific. And “more effectively” is the real test. A chart has to beat the alternative, which is often just reading a table.

An older and more poetic version comes from the French cartographer Jacques Bertin, who described graphics as finding the “artificial memory” that best supports our natural means of perception [Bertin 1967]. A picture lets you store information outside your head and pull it back in with your eyes.

Why pictures work: perception versus cognition

Your brain has two very different ways of handling information.

Perception is fast, automatic and parallel. You see that one dot is red, or that a cloud of points slopes upwards, before you’ve decided to look. Cognition is slow and effortful. It is reading numbers one by one, holding them in memory and comparing them.

The first time, you have to scan letter by letter. With the V’s in red, you count them at a glance. Nothing about the data changed. The task moved from cognition to perception.

That is the whole game in one sentence, and the course repeated it often: replace cognition with perception.

Same numbers, different stories

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets [Anscombe 1973]. They have the same mean, the same variance, the same correlation and the same regression line. On paper they are identical. Plotted, one is a clean straight line, one is a curve, one is a straight line knocked off by a single outlier, and one is a vertical stack of points with one point far away.

In 2017 Justin Matejka and George Fitzmaurice took the idea further with the Datasaurus Dozen: thirteen datasets with the same summary statistics to two decimal places, one of which is a drawing of a dinosaur [Matejka and Fitzmaurice 2017]. The lesson is the one on the course slides. Summaries can lose information. Details matter.

Why we visualize at all

The course listed several reasons, and they’re worth knowing separately:

  • To record information. A seismograph is a visualization that writes itself.
  • To augment human abilities. Many real problems are badly defined. You don’t know what you’re looking for until you see it.
  • To generate new questions. What do my data look like? Can I trust this model?
  • To confirm a hypothesis. In 1854 the physician John Snow plotted cholera deaths on a street map of London. The deaths clustered around one water pump on Broad Street. At the time most people believed cholera spread through bad air, and the map was strong evidence that it came from contaminated water [Snow 1855; see the Wikipedia article on the outbreak].
  • To communicate. People rate well-designed figures as more interesting and more rigorous, and highly cited scientific papers tend to have more diagrams per page.

The human in the loop

You might ask why we don’t just let computers find the answers. Sometimes we should. The course was blunt about it: visualization is not needed when there is a trustworthy, time-saving automatic solution. A spam filter doesn’t need a chart.

But automated analysis only works reliably for problems that are well defined and well understood [Keim et al. 2008]. Real data is messy, and different models can tell different stories about the same numbers. A person looking at the data brings knowledge the algorithm doesn’t have. They can notice the thing nobody told them to look for. Keim’s phrase for this is that the human can “detect the expected and discover the unexpected.”

So “human in the loop” means a cycle. The computer shows something. The person reads it, forms an idea, and acts by filtering, selecting or changing a setting. The computer updates. Round and round. Visualization augments people. It doesn’t replace them.

2. From data to picture

The short version. Every chart goes through the same few steps. You clean the data, pick the part you care about, decide which visual property shows which number, and then draw it. Each step involves choices, and those choices shape what people end up seeing.

The visualization pipeline

The course described the pipeline as a chain of data products, each made by one step:

  1. Raw data goes through data analysis and comes out as processed, cleaned data.
  2. Filtering picks out the focus data, the part you actually want to show.
  3. Mapping turns data into marks and visual properties: points, lines, colors, sizes.
  4. Rendering turns those into pixels.

Two things sit on top of the whole chain. The domain gives meaning to the data. Are the numbers 5001, 5007 and 5009 quantities, IDs or postal codes? You can’t tell from the numbers. The task is the question you’re trying to answer.

One warning from the slides stuck with me. Decisions in processing and filtering already shape what you see, and they are not automatically objective.

An example from my own work

My first programming exercise was a world map of sunshine. The data was a spreadsheet of monthly hours of sunshine for 382 cities in 140 countries, one row per city and one column per month. Walking through the pipeline:

  • Analysis. I reshaped the table from “wide” to “long”, one row per city and month, and fixed country names so they matched the map file. “United States” in the data had to become “United States of America” in the map. I also divided monthly hours by the number of days in the month, so February wasn’t penalized for being short.
  • Filtering. A slider picked one month at a time.
  • Mapping. Each country became a filled shape on an Equal Earth projection, colored from black for little sun to yellow for a lot.
  • Rendering. Observable Plot drew it, with a legend and tooltips.

It looked good. It was also wrong in a way I only noticed much later. The data had many cities per country, 54 of them for the United States, and my code silently kept the last one in the list for each country. The whole US was colored by Yuma, Arizona, one of the sunniest places on Earth, at about 4,000 hours a year. The average of the 54 US cities is closer to 2,800. A processing decision I didn’t think about changed the picture. The fix is to average the cities per country, or to drop the country shapes and draw one dot per city.

That is exactly the warning on the slide, and I walked straight into it.

What kind of data is it?

Before you can draw anything, you need to know what kind of data you have. Munzner groups datasets into a few types [Munzner 2014]:

  • Tables. Rows are items, columns are attributes. A spreadsheet.
  • Networks. Things connected to other things. Friends, flights, web pages.
  • Trees. A network with a hierarchy, where each child has exactly one parent. Folders on your computer.
  • Fields. Continuous measurements sampled on a grid. Temperature across a region, or density inside a body in a CT scan.
  • Geometry. Shapes with positions. Country borders, roads.

The first three are abstract. You choose where things go on the page. The last two are spatial. Their positions come from the real world, so position is already taken.

The columns themselves come in types too. Categorical data has no order, like dog breeds. Ordinal data has an order but no meaningful distance, like small, medium and large. Quantitative data is numbers you can do arithmetic with, like weight. Ordered data also has a direction. It can be sequential, from low to high. It can be diverging, around a meaningful middle like zero. Or it can be cyclic, like months or hours of the day.

One more distinction does a lot of work later. A key is an attribute you look things up by. It is independent and tells you which item you’re looking at. A value is what you look up. It is dependent and tells you how much. In a table of penguin counts per species, the species is the key and the count is the value.

Marks and channels

Every chart can be taken apart into marks and channels. A mark is the geometric thing that represents an item: a point, a line, an area. A channel is a visual property that encodes an attribute: position, length, color, size, shape.

A scatterplot uses point marks with horizontal and vertical position. A bar chart uses line marks, which surprises people, with length encoding the value. A heatmap uses area marks with color.

This sounds academic, but it’s the most useful tool in the field. Once you see charts as marks and channels, you can describe any chart, compare two charts, and design new ones.

Some channels are better than others

In 1984 William Cleveland and Robert McGill asked people to compare values shown in different ways and measured how accurate they were [Cleveland and McGill 1984]. The ranking that came out of it, refined later, is roughly this:

  1. Position along a common scale
  2. Position on separate but identical scales
  3. Length
  4. Angle and slope
  5. Area
  6. Volume, color intensity

This is why bar charts beat pie charts for precise comparison, and why most good charts put the important number on a position axis.

Two principles guide the choice. Expressiveness means the chart shows all of the data and only the data. Don’t draw a line between categories that have no order. Effectiveness means the most important attribute gets the most effective channel. Use magnitude channels like position and length for ordered data, and identity channels like hue and shape for categories.

The most famous chart

Charles Minard’s 1869 map of Napoleon’s Russian campaign is often called the best statistical graphic ever drawn [Tufte 1983]. It encodes six variables at once. The width of the band is the size of the army. Its position is the route across the map. Its color separates the advance from the retreat. A line chart along the bottom shows the temperature during the retreat, with dates. You can read the destruction of an army of over 400,000 men down to about 10,000 without reading a single number. Wikipedia has a good reproduction.

A test for whether a chart is “grammatically correct”

Gordon Kindlmann and Carlos Scheidegger proposed a neat way to check a design [Kindlmann and Scheidegger 2014]. Their question is simple: when the data changes in an important way, does the picture change in an obvious and meaningful way? They break it into three principles:

  1. Representation invariance. The same data should always give the same picture. If shuffling the rows of your spreadsheet changes your chart, you have created a pattern from nothing. They call that a hallucinator.
  2. Unambiguous data depiction. If the data changes in an important way, the picture must change visibly. If it doesn’t, the change is hidden. That’s a confuser.
  3. Visual-data correspondence. The size and kind of visual change should match the data change. A big data change shown as a tiny visual one is a jumbler. A tiny data change shown as a dramatic visual one is a misleader. Their example is elevation above and below sea level on a diverging color scale. Flip the data’s sign and the two colors swap, which matches what happened.

They call it algebraic because they write these rules as equations about data changes and visual changes. You don’t need the math to use it. It works as a checklist.

3. How your eyes and brain read a chart

The short version. Some things jump out at you instantly, like one red dot among grey ones. Other things take effort, like comparing two bars at opposite ends of a chart. Good design puts the important thing where your eyes catch it for free.

Things that pop out

Look at a field of grey dots with one red dot. You find the red one immediately, and it takes the same time whether there are ten dots or a thousand. This is called preattentive processing. It happens in under about 200 milliseconds, and the course slide made a point I like: despite the name, it still requires attention. What matters is contrast. The red dot pops out because it is different from its neighbors, not because red is special.

Features that pop out include color, size, orientation, line length and width, motion and flicker. Combinations don’t. Finding the one red square among red circles and blue squares means checking items one at a time, because the target differs only by the combination of color and shape.

For design, this means picking one feature to make the most important thing stand out. Highlight everything and nothing stands out.

Grouping: Gestalt principles

Gestalt is German for “form”. The idea from Gestalt psychology is that we see wholes, not parts, and we group things automatically:

  • Proximity. Things close together look like a group.
  • Similarity. Things with the same color or shape look like they belong together.
  • Symmetry. Symmetric shapes read as one object.
  • Parallelism. Parallel lines group.
  • Figure and ground. We split the scene into a foreground object and a background.

Charts depend on these. Bars next to each other get compared first. Points of the same color read as one series. Heavy gridlines are bad partly because they compete with the data for the role of “figure”.

One nuance from the course: researchers have found these principles vary across cultures, so they are partly learned, not universal laws of the eye.

Quick impressions versus careful comparisons

Steven Franconeri and colleagues reviewed decades of research on how people read charts [Franconeri et al. 2021]. One finding is that visual statistics are fast. At a glance you can tell the average size of a crowd of bubbles, the range, the overall trend. Comparisons are slow. “This bar is taller than that one” needs your eyes to jump between the two, and you can only make a few comparisons at a time.

So the designer has to guide the comparison. If you want people to compare A and B, put them next to each other on a common baseline. Position and proximity are strong cues. Giving two bars the same color while placing them far apart is weak.

Franconeri also warns about the “curse of knowledge”. Once you know the pattern in your chart, you can’t un-see it, and you assume your audience sees it too. They often don’t. Highlight it and write it down.

The trouble with pie charts

Pie charts are designed to show proportions through angle. But when Drew Skau and Robert Kosara tested pie and donut charts where only one property carried the data, people seemed to read the pies mostly by arc length and area, not by angle [Skau and Kosara 2016]. All three are less accurate than comparing lengths.

The course’s balanced verdict was that pie charts aren’t always bad. They’re fine for showing parts of a whole when there are fewer than five slices. For anything that needs precise comparison, use bars.

4. Choosing the right chart

The short version. The right chart depends on two things: what your data looks like, and what question you’re asking. Count the categories you’re splitting by, decide what you want people to compare, and the choice mostly makes itself.

Start with the task

Munzner describes tasks as pairs of an action and a target [Munzner 2014]. The action is what you’re doing. The target is what you’re doing it to.

Actions come in levels. At the top you analyze, which means consuming information or producing new information. In the middle you search. At the bottom you query: identify one thing, compare some things, or summarize all of them.

Search has a tidy two-by-two structure that I find useful in everyday life:

  • You know what you want and where it is: lookup. Finding a word in a dictionary.
  • You know what you want but not where: locate. Finding a friend’s name in a big network diagram.
  • You don’t know what you want, but you know where to look: browse. Scanning the top of a sorted list.
  • You know neither: explore. Looking at a new scatterplot to see if anything is there.

Targets can be the whole dataset, with trends, outliers and features. Or one attribute, with its distribution and extremes. Or relationships between attributes, like correlation.

Four ways to arrange a table

Most everyday charts show tables. The course’s lab on arranging tables gave four design choices, and the number of keys mostly decides which one you use.

  1. Express values. Zero keys. Just place each item where its values say. With two quantitative values, that is a scatterplot.
  2. Separate, order and align regions. One or more keys. Give each category its own region of space, put the regions in a sensible order, and line them up on a common baseline. With one categorical key and one value, that is a bar chart.
  3. Axis orientation. Rectilinear, with ordinary x and y axes. Parallel, as in parallel coordinates. Or radial, as in pie and radar charts.
  4. Layout density. Dense layouts shrink marks down to single pixels to fit huge amounts of data. Space-filling layouts use every pixel, like treemaps.

Bars or lines?

A line suggests that the thing on the horizontal axis is continuous or at least ordered, so you can read between the points. Lines between categories like “apples, oranges, bananas” imply a trend that doesn’t exist. Bars say “these are separate”. The question to ask is whether the data’s meaning matches what the chart implies.

A histogram looks like a bar chart but isn’t one. A bar chart counts things per category. A histogram first cuts a continuous number into bins, then counts per bin, so the bin width is a design choice that can hide or create patterns.

Sketch before you build

For my team’s project Oslo on Two Wheels, a data story about Oslo’s city bikes, we used the Five Design-Sheet method by Jonathan Roberts and colleagues [Roberts et al. 2016]. You start with a sheet of quick brainstorm sketches, then draw three different principal designs, then refine one into a final design. It forces you to consider alternatives before writing any code, and it’s cheap. Paper is faster than a framework.

5. Honest and dishonest charts

The short version. A chart can be built from true numbers and still mislead you. The usual tricks are cutting off the bottom of an axis, leaving out context, and picking colors or scales that exaggerate. The opposite mistake exists too. Sometimes the “safe” choice hides what matters.

Tufte’s lie factor

Edward Tufte defined a simple measure of honesty [Tufte 1983]:

Lie factor = size of the effect shown in the graphic ÷ size of the effect in the data

A lie factor of about 1 is honest. If sales grew 10% and the bars make it look like 300%, the lie factor is 30. Tufte’s broader principle is graphical integrity: show data variation, not design variation, and give context.

Cutting off the axis

The classic trick is truncating the y-axis so it doesn’t start at zero. Franconeri and colleagues describe a real example: a chart of health insurance sign-ups that showed 6 million against a target of 7 million [Franconeri et al. 2021]. The real ratio is 6 to 7. The truncated bars made it look closer to 1 to 3.

Michael Correll and colleagues tested this directly. People judged differences as bigger in truncated charts, and the effect held up even when the charts carried a broken-axis symbol [Correll et al. 2020]. Labeling the truncation doesn’t undo it.

But zero isn’t always honest either

Correll also wrote about the opposite case, a global temperature chart drawn on an axis from zero degrees Fahrenheit see his essay “An inconvenient graph”. Its lie factor is about 1. It also makes global warming look like a flat line, which hides a change of a degree or two that has enormous consequences. The more truthful version shows temperature anomalies on a truncated axis, with the uncertainty drawn in.

So the course didn’t give a rule. It gave three questions:

  1. Take active responsibility for choosing the visual size of the effect.
  2. Ask what counts as an important change in this domain.
  3. Ask whether zero is a meaningful landmark. For counts, money and sign-ups it is. For temperatures in Fahrenheit it isn’t.

The chart type matters too. A bar’s length encodes its value, so a truncated bar lies about its own length. A line chart encodes change through position and slope, so truncating it is easier to defend.

Leaving out the whole picture

One lecture example was a news graphic saying “21 million Americans believe both” that Biden is illegitimate and that force is justified to restore Trump. The number came from a real poll. The same poll showed 74% of people agreed with neither, roughly 191 million. The headline isn’t false. It just shows the scary slice without the whole.

Context is often missing by accident rather than malice. My sunshine map from section 2 is an example. Nobody was trying to mislead anyone. I just didn’t know the data had many cities per country.

I built a deceptive chart on purpose

The second programming exercise asked us to make an honest chart and then a deliberately misleading one from the same data. I used the IMDB top 1000 films and computed, per genre, the gap between audience ratings and critic scores.

The honest version was a diverging bar chart around zero, sorted by the gap. Blue meant audiences liked the genre more, red meant critics did. The number of films behind each bar was printed on it, and a caption warned that some genres had very few films.

The deceptive version used the same numbers and changed only design decisions:

  • I sorted by the size of the gap and ignored its direction, and titled it “Which genres are most controversial?”
  • I dropped the sample sizes.
  • I colored bars like a traffic light, green, yellow, red, by gap size, which hid which side liked the genre more.

Film-Noir, with three films, and Thriller, with one, ended up looking like the most controversial genres in cinema. Every number on the chart was true.

Doing this changed how I read charts. Every encoding parameter and data transformation is a place where honesty can leak out.

6. Color

The short version. Color is really three things: lightness, colorfulness and the type of color. Lightness and colorfulness are good for “how much”. The type of color, red versus green versus blue, is good for “which kind”. The popular rainbow color scale breaks both rules, and about one in twelve men can’t tell some of its colors apart.

Three channels, not one

Color has three separate channels:

  • Luminance is how light or dark something is.
  • Saturation is how vivid it is, from grey to full color.
  • Hue is what we usually mean by “color”: red, green, blue.

Luminance and saturation have a natural order. Darker can mean more. So they’re magnitude channels, good for ordered and quantitative data. Hue has no natural order. Is green more than red? So hue is an identity channel, good for categories.

Keep the number of colors small. The course said three to nine steps is ideal, because the viewer has to hold the mapping in working memory while reading the chart.

What makes a good color scale

For continuous data you need a colormap, a smooth gradient from low to high. Two properties matter most:

  1. Luminance that only goes one way. From dark to light, never back down. Then the order survives when the chart is printed in greyscale, and for most color-blind readers.
  2. Perceptual uniformity. Equal steps in the data should look like equal steps in color. Researchers measure this in color spaces built from experiments on human vision, where a distance of one roughly means one just-noticeable difference.

The viridis colormap, from purple through blue and green to yellow, was designed to satisfy both, and it became matplotlib’s default for that reason [van der Walt and Smith 2015].

Why the rainbow scale is a problem

The rainbow, or “jet”, colormap fails both tests. Its luminance goes up from dark blue to bright yellow and then down again to dark red, so in greyscale the high and low ends look the same. Its perceptual steps are uneven. Some stretches barely change while others, around yellow and cyan, jump. Readers see sharp boundaries in data that changes smoothly.

The course showed a map of sea floor depth off Nova Scotia where a band of color made one area look like a pit. The feature came from the color scale, not from the sea floor.

Still, the course didn’t ban the rainbow. Colin Ware, Maureen Stone and Danielle Albers Szafir argue that rainbow maps are not all bad [Ware, Stone and Szafir 2023]. Because their hues are easy to name, they’re good for reading off a specific value using a key, and they can be redesigned to be perceptually uniform.

Color also carries meaning. Blue for cold and red for hot is so familiar that reversing it costs the reader effort for no gain.

Color blindness

We see color with three types of cone cells in the eye, sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths. Roughly speaking, blue, green and red. When one type is missing or works differently, some colors become hard to tell apart. The most common forms are red-green deficiencies. Protanopia affects the long-wavelength cones and deuteranopia the medium ones. They affect roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women of Northern European ancestry.

Designing around it is mostly about not relying on hue alone:

  • Avoid pairing red with green, and purple with blue.
  • Use a redundant channel. Vary luminance as well as hue, or add shapes, patterns or direct labels.
  • Use palettes made for this, like the color-blind-safe schemes in ColorBrewer.
  • Check your chart with a color-blindness simulator, and in greyscale.

A colormap with steadily increasing luminance, like viridis, passes most of these checks automatically.

7. Interaction

The short version. A static chart tells you something. An interactive one lets you ask it things. You can filter, zoom, hover for details, or click something in one chart and watch it light up in another. Interaction also costs time and attention, and most people never use it, so it has to be worth it.

Why make it interactive?

The course summed it up as “visualization shows the user, interaction tells the computer”. The arguments for interaction:

  • It engages people.
  • It manages complexity. You can’t show everything at once, so you let people filter and drill down.
  • It allows hypothesis testing on the fly. Change a parameter and see what happens.
  • It can record what you did, so you can retrace your steps.

The arguments against were on a slide called “Interaction trade-offs”. Interaction costs time. It adds cognitive load. Many users never interact at all. The slide cited a figure of around 90% of readers not interacting, from Gregor Aisch’s 2017 essay on interactive graphics at the New York Times [Aisch 2017]. And there’s a tension between visible controls, which take space, and invisible ones like hover, which nobody discovers.

Christian Tominski and Heidrun Schumann put the balance into one rule they call the efficiency principle: “The gains from using an interactive visual approach should outweigh the computational resources and human effort necessary to carry out the analysis” [Tominski and Schumann 2020].

The mantra

Ben Shneiderman’s visual information-seeking mantra is probably the most quoted sentence in the field [Shneiderman 1996]:

Overview first, zoom and filter, then details-on-demand.

Show everything at a coarse level. Let people narrow it down. Give the full details of one item only when they ask.

My third programming exercise followed it closely. The question was which actors appear together in films, using the IMDB top 1000.

  • Overview. A chord diagram of all actors, with ribbons for shared films, next to a grid of movie posters.
  • Zoom and filter. A year range, a slider for the minimum number of shared films, and a sort menu. Clicking a ribbon between two actors filtered the poster grid to their shared films. Clicking a poster did the reverse.
  • Details on demand. Clicking a poster showed a panel with the film’s stars.

Films that were filtered out didn’t disappear. They faded to low opacity, so you could still see the selection against the whole collection. That is technically highlighting rather than filtering, and it was a deliberate choice.

In the lab before that exercise, we built the same ideas with dropdowns and sliders that update a chart instantly. These are often called dynamic queries. One small lesson from that lab: when you filter, keep the axes fixed. If the scales jump every time the data changes, you can’t compare one filter setting to the next.

A taxonomy of interactions

Jeffrey Heer and Ben Shneiderman sorted interactions into three groups, with four verbs each [Heer and Shneiderman 2012]. I remember them as specify, manipulate, remember.

  • Data and view specification is about what to show: visualize, filter, sort, derive.
  • View manipulation is about how to look at it: select, navigate, coordinate, organize.
  • Process and provenance is about keeping track: record, annotate, share, guide.

An undo history is “record”. A link to a saved dashboard state is “share”. A guided tour through a story is “guide”.

Several views at once

Splitting data across several views is called faceting. The case for it fits in two words: eyes beat memory. Comparing two things side by side is easier than remembering one while looking at the other. Munzner describes three ways to do it [Munzner 2014]:

  • Juxtapose. Place views side by side. Small multiples, Tufte’s term for a grid of small charts with the same axes, are the classic example.
  • Partition. Split the data by the categories of an attribute, like one chart per passenger class on the Titanic.
  • Superimpose. Layer views on top of each other, the way Google Maps layers traffic over streets.

The costs are less space per view, eye movement between views, and clutter when too many layers pile up.

Linked highlighting

When views are shown together, they can be linked. Select some items in one view and the same items light up in all the others. This is also called brushing and linking. The views don’t need to look alike. A histogram of temperatures can be linked to a 3D model of an engine, and brushing the hottest bars shows where on the engine they are.

Each view only shows a few attributes, and the highlight ties them together. That’s what makes it powerful for data with many dimensions. A scatterplot matrix with linked brushing is the textbook example. Select a cluster in one small plot and see where those points fall in every other pair of attributes.

Focus and context

“See the trees without missing the forest.” Focus plus context means showing the part you care about in full detail while the rest stays visible around it, so you don’t lose your bearings. The course’s example was a stock price chart. A large chart shows the selected months, and a thin strip below shows the whole timeline with a draggable window marking where you are.

The course contrasted this with overview plus detail, shown as a dashboard with several levels: an overview strip, a more detailed chart, and a small table for the selected item. Each level has its own encoding.

You’ll find textbooks that draw the line differently, calling the stock chart pattern overview plus detail and keeping “focus plus context” for single distorted views like a fisheye lens. The underlying idea is the same either way. Keep the context on screen while you zoom in.

8. Networks and trees

The short version. A network is things connected to other things: friends, flights, web links. You can draw it as dots and lines, or as a grid where each filled cell means “these two are connected”. Each works best for different questions. Trees, like the folders on your computer, are a special kind of network, and they have their own set of tricks.

What people want from a network

Network tasks come in two kinds. Attribute tasks are about values on the nodes and links, like the busiest airport or the heaviest connection. Topology tasks are about the structure itself: paths, neighbors, central nodes, communities.

Both kinds split into three goals:

Attribute-basedTopology-based
Localize, find things with a propertyThe link with the highest weightAll neighbors of a node
Quantify, count or estimateThe number of nodesHow many links point into a node
Sort or orderSort links by weightFollow a path from a starting node

Dots and lines

The familiar drawing is the node-link diagram. A good layout tries to:

  • minimize edge crossings, node overlaps and bends in edges
  • keep connected nodes close and edge lengths roughly equal
  • spread out the angles between edges meeting at a node
  • draw similar structures the same way, which is symmetry
  • use a reasonable amount of space

These goals fight each other. Fewer crossings often means more space. Symmetry and equal edge lengths can’t always both be had. There’s no perfect layout, only trade-offs.

Two algorithms came up again and again.

Force-directed layout treats the network as a physics simulation. Edges are springs that pull connected nodes together. Every node pushes every other node away, like charged particles. Start from random positions, run the simulation until it settles, and clusters of tightly connected nodes tend to pull together on their own. It is flexible and often looks good. It is also non-deterministic, so you get a different picture each run. It’s slow for big networks, because every node pushes on every other node, though tricks like grouping distant nodes in a quadtree help. And viewers read meaning into distances that have none.

The Sugiyama layout, from Kozo Sugiyama and colleagues in 1981, is for networks with a direction, like a flowchart or a family tree [Sugiyama et al. 1981]. It assigns each node to a layer by its depth, reorders nodes within each layer to reduce crossings, then sets final positions. The result reads cleanly from top to bottom. It’s no use for networks without a natural hierarchy. The GraphViz tool uses this approach.

The grid view: adjacency matrices

The other main drawing is the adjacency matrix. Every node gets a row and a column, and a filled cell means the two are connected. Color can show how strong the connection is.

It looks abstract at first, but it has real advantages. There are no crossing lines, so it stays readable for dense networks where a node-link diagram turns into a hairball. You can answer “are A and B connected?” by looking up one cell. Groups of tightly connected nodes show up as dark blocks along the diagonal, if the rows are in the right order. Mike Bostock’s co-occurrence matrix of Les Misérables characters lets you reorder the rows and watch the clusters appear and vanish.

The main weakness is paths. Following a route from A to C through B is natural in a node-link diagram and painful in a matrix. NodeTrix combines both: a node-link diagram for the overall structure, with dense clusters drawn as small matrices [Henry, Fekete and McGuffin 2007].

Oslo on Two Wheels, a data story about Oslo’s city bikes that I built with my team, has an origin-destination matrix of bike trips. Areas of the city run along both axes, and each cell is colored by the number of trips between them. Bike trips form a dense, weighted network, which is exactly what matrices handle well.

Flows between places

Chord diagrams and Sankey diagrams are two more options when the links carry an amount, like money or people moving between places. Both have trouble when flows go both ways between the same pair, which is the normal case for migration.

The kriskogram, introduced by Ningchuan Xiao and Yongwan Chun for migration data, handles that directly [Xiao and Chun 2009]. All places sit in a row along one horizontal axis. Each flow is a semicircular arc between two places. Arcs above the axis go in one direction and arcs below go in the other, and the width of an arc shows how many people moved. Direction is visible without arrowheads, and both directions between any pair stay visible at once.

In the original paper kriskograms were static and black and white, and they’ve seen little use since. For a seminar project in my master’s I built an interactive kriskogram explorer using US state-to-state migration data from the Census Bureau. I drew on interaction ideas from a survey of geospatial network visualization by Sarah Schöttler and colleagues [Schöttler et al. 2021]. Two features came out of it:

  • Ego focus picks one state and filters the view to its neighbors, one or more steps out. Selecting California, for example, leaves only the flows into and out of California.
  • Temporal overlay draws several years on top of each other, with color for the year. In an animated mode, dashes move along each arc in the direction of the flow, and their speed can follow the size of the flow.

A kriskogram takes some training to read, like an adjacency matrix. Once you know the above-and-below rule, patterns like a state that mostly loses people to its neighbors stand out quickly.

Trees

The course described four main ways to draw a tree.

Indentation is the file browser. Each item gets a row, and its depth is shown by how far it is pushed to the right. It’s great for reading labels and terrible for seeing the overall shape of a big tree.

Node-link drawings of trees have a classic algorithm, the Reingold-Tilford “tidy tree” [Reingold and Tilford 1981]. Its rules are to put nodes of the same depth on the same level, avoid crossings, pack subtrees as tightly as possible, draw identical subtrees identically, and center each parent over its children. The catch is that trees usually get wider very fast, so you need zooming, filtering, collapsing branches or a different layout.

Enclosure puts children inside their parent’s rectangle. The best-known version is the treemap, invented by Ben Shneiderman [Shneiderman 1992], where the area of each rectangle shows a quantity like file size. Treemaps use every pixel, but in a flat treemap it’s hard to see how deeply things are nested. Jarke van Wijk and Huub van de Wetering’s cushion treemaps fix that with shading that makes each level look like a raised cushion [van Wijk and van de Wetering 1999]. If you’ve ever used WinDirStat to find what’s eating your hard drive, you’ve used a cushion treemap.

Layering stacks children next to their parent in rows or rings. An icicle plot hangs levels below each other. A sunburst bends the same thing into rings. Programmers know a version of this as the flame graph, where each layer is a function call and its width is the time spent. I’ve used flame graphs to find out why a web page rendered slowly. The hierarchy shows which component triggered which slow function, which a flat list of timings can’t.

9. Time

The short version. Time is special because it only goes one way and it repeats. Days, weeks and seasons come back around. You can show time by animating a chart, which feels natural but makes comparison hard. Or you can lay time out along an axis, which takes a moment to read but lets you compare any two moments directly.

Space versus time

Almost every measurement only makes sense with a where and a when. The price of a laptop depends on the shop and on the date. But space and time behave differently. You can walk back to where you came from. You can’t go back to yesterday. Time also has natural cycles, like hours, weekdays and seasons, that space usually doesn’t. A good time visualization respects both properties, the direction and the cycles.

Questions people ask about time

Alan MacEachren listed seven basic questions you can ask of time-oriented data [MacEachren 1995]. They form a natural chain:

  1. Existence. Does it exist? Was a measurement made in July 1960?
  2. Temporal location. When does it exist?
  3. Time interval. How long does it last?
  4. Temporal texture. How often does it happen?
  5. Rate of change. How fast is it changing?
  6. Sequence. In what order do things happen? Did the explosion happen before or after the crash?
  7. Synchronization. Do things happen together? Is my birthday on Easter Monday this year?

A design that’s good for “how often” might be useless for “in what order”, so it helps to know which question you’re answering.

Two ways to show time

Wolfgang Aigner and colleagues describe two basic mappings [Aigner et al. 2011].

Dynamic: time shown as time. Animation. It’s probably the most natural mapping, because there’s no conversion in your head, and it’s good for following movement and trends. But you can’t directly compare two moments. To compare this frame with one ten seconds ago, you have to remember the old one.

Static: time shown as space. Time goes on an axis or another visual property, and everything is on screen at once. You have to read time as space, but you can compare any two moments directly.

The course’s conclusion was that animation is used surprisingly little in practice, and more interactivity is needed. A slider that lets you step through time yourself sits in between. My sunshine map used a month slider, so you could flip back and forth between months to compare them.

Which visual properties can carry time?

  • Position is the most common and the most accurate. Time goes on the x-axis.
  • Length shows duration, as in a timeline or a Gantt chart.
  • Angle works for clock-like, cyclic views.
  • Connection, with lines or arrows, shows order, from “before” to “after”.
  • Labels mark specific dates, often combined with connections.
  • Brightness can show age, with older items fading into the background.

Area, shape and texture work poorly for time.

The space-time cube

What if you need both where and when? The space-time cube, an idea that goes back to the geographer Torsten Hägerstrand, puts a map on the floor and runs time straight up. A moving person or vehicle becomes a line climbing through the cube. Standing still makes a vertical line. Moving fast makes a nearly flat one. If two lines touch, two things were in the same place at the same time.

The course’s example was GeoTime, which uses this to analyze movements [Kapler and Wright 2004]. In the example, investigators looking into a hit-and-run used the GPS tracks of four taxis to work out which one could have been at the scene. The cost is the usual cost of 3D: things hide behind each other, perspective distorts distances, and you need to rotate the view to read it well.

10. Maps

The short version. You can’t flatten a globe without stretching it, just as you can’t flatten an orange peel without tearing it. Every world map distorts something: sizes, distances, directions or shapes. Which map is right depends on what you need. And the most popular kind of data map, where countries are colored by a number, has traps of its own.

Two kinds of maps

A reference map is about where things are: roads, rivers, borders. Accuracy is critical, because people use it for zoning, taxes, construction and navigation.

A thematic map puts data on top of a reference map to show how something is spread out over space. Snow’s cholera map from section 1 is a thematic map. So is every election map you’ve seen.

Projections: you can’t keep everything

A projection turns latitude and longitude on a sphere into x and y on a flat surface. It always distorts at least one of four things:

  • Area. Are sizes correct?
  • Bearing. Are compass directions correct?
  • Distance. Are distances correct?
  • Shape. Do shapes look right locally?

You can’t keep both shape and area. That trade-off is the core of map design.

Projections come in three families, named after the shape you imagine wrapping around the globe before unrolling it:

Cylindrical, a tube around the globe. The Mercator projection from 1569 is the famous one. It keeps compass bearings as straight lines, so a sailor can follow a constant heading drawn on the map. It also keeps local shapes. The price is area, which grows enormously towards the poles. Greenland looks about the size of Africa, but Africa is roughly fourteen times bigger. Mercator is great for navigation and a poor choice for a world map of data.

Conic, a cone set on the globe like a hat. These suit countries in the middle latitudes. The Albers projection keeps areas correct and is common for maps of the United States. Shapes and distances are close to right near two chosen lines of latitude and drift further away.

Azimuthal, a flat sheet touching the globe at one point. The azimuthal equidistant projection keeps distances and directions from that central point correct, which is useful for drawing how far a plane can fly from one airport. The gnomonic projection turns great circles, the shortest routes over the globe, into straight lines.

Seeing the distortion

In the 1800s Nicolas Tissot came up with a way to make distortion visible. Draw identical small circles all over the globe and project them. On Mercator the circles stay round, because shapes are preserved, but they swell towards the poles, because area isn’t. On an equal-area map they all keep the same area but get squashed into ellipses. The circles are known as Tissot’s indicatrix.

The choropleth map and its traps

A choropleth map colors each region by a value, like unemployment per county. It’s everywhere, and it has two well-known hazards.

Spurious correlations. Many raw counts just follow population. A map of total cases of almost anything looks like a map of where people live. The xkcd comic “Heatmap” makes this joke perfectly. The fix is to normalize, for example per 100 people.

Big regions shout. A big region takes up a lot of ink however few people live in it. On a US map, Montana dominates the eye even if very few people live there. Datawrapper has a good guide to doing choropleths well.

The course’s “best case” list was one variable at a time, normalized where it makes sense, a careful choice of regions and colors, and regions of roughly equal size. I’d add an equal-area projection, so that Mercator doesn’t make Scandinavia look more important than it is. My sunshine map used Equal Earth for exactly that reason.

Alternatives to the choropleth

  • Symbol maps draw a symbol, usually a circle, at each place, sized by the value. The real geography stays in the background, and big regions no longer dominate. Size the circles so their area matches the value, not their radius. Our city bike project used this for stations. Overlapping symbols are the main problem.
  • Cartograms stretch the geography itself so that each region’s area matches its value, like a world map where countries are sized by population. They are striking but hard to read unless you already know the geography well.
  • Grid cartograms turn every region into an equal tile, placed roughly where it belongs. US election maps often use one square per state. Size bias disappears, along with recognizable shapes.
  • Grid maps, like hexagon bins, divide the area into equal cells and color them by the data inside.
  • Dot density maps place one dot per fixed number of things, like one dot per 100 people. They’re good for showing clusters, though they still need normalizing.

11. Visual analytics

The short version. Visual analytics is what you do when the data is too big and messy for either a person or an algorithm alone. The computer crunches, the person looks and steers, and the two take turns. It works like a loop: show something, select what looks interesting, see where else it shows up, and repeat with sharper tools.

Why combine humans and algorithms

Daniel Keim and colleagues define visual analytics as combining automated analysis with interactive visualization [Keim et al. 2008]. Their argument is that automated methods only work reliably for problems that are well defined and well understood. Real data, especially from simulations and measurements, is multi-faceted. Different models can fit the same data equally well and still tell different stories about it. The statistician Leo Breiman called this the Rashomon effect, after the film where witnesses give conflicting accounts of the same event [Breiman 2001]. Visual analytics keeps a person in the loop to judge which story holds up.

The course called this visual data science, and said it shines on data that is big, has many attributes per item, and often has a spatial or temporal side. Simulation output is the typical case: millions of grid cells, each with temperature, pressure, velocity and more, over many time steps.

The running example: an engine’s cooling jacket

The lectures used a simulation of coolant flowing through the cooling jacket of a car engine. Every cell of the 3D model has a temperature, a velocity and other values. Engineers want to know where it gets dangerously hot, and why.

Four nested loops

Helwig Hauser’s group at the University of Bergen describes interactive visual analysis as four levels of increasingly powerful selection [Muigg et al. 2008]. They are nested loops, with level one innermost. Whatever a deeper level produces gets fed back into level one.

Level one, show and brush. The tightest loop. Show a view, brush a selection, and see it highlighted in linked views. Brush the hottest bars in a temperature histogram, and the 3D engine model lights up where those cells are, with the rest of the engine faded into context. Brushing can go three ways. Brushing a location and asking what values are there is local investigation. Brushing values and asking where they occur is feature localization. Relating several attributes to each other is multivariate analysis.

Level two, between the lines. Combine brushes with logic: AND, OR and subtract. “Hot AND slow-moving” finds the places where coolant sits still and heats up. Combining brushes this way builds up a precise description of a feature, sometimes called a feature definition language [Doleisch, Gasser and Hauser 2003].

Level three, dive deeper. Some interesting things aren’t in any column of the data. They’re implicit. Level three makes them explicit by deriving new attributes and then brushing those. The slides paired each user goal with a method:

What you want to seeHow to derive it
Relative valuesNormalization
ChangeDerivatives, like a temperature gradient
Properties that depend on more than one pointIntegration along a path or over a region
Statistical propertiesDescriptive statistics, like the mean or spread over time per cell
Ratios or differencesRatios and differences
Anything elseData mining, like clustering or embeddings

For example, if each cell has 100 time steps of temperature, you can compute each cell’s standard deviation over time and brush the cells that fluctuate most. Those might suffer from thermal stress even if their average looks fine.

Level three also has smarter brushes. Angular brushing selects lines in a parallel coordinates plot by their slope [Hauser, Ledermann and Doleisch 2002]. Similarity brushing lets you sketch a curve and select every curve that looks like it [Muigg et al. 2008].

Level four, application-specific features. At the deepest level the tools are built for one domain. The course’s example was AVOCLA, an aortic vortex classification for cardiologists [Meuschke et al. 2016]. Doctors suspect that swirling blood flow in the aorta is linked to heart disease, but they have classified vortices by hand, using properties that aren’t standardized. AVOCLA clusters the path lines that make up each vortex and classifies them semi-automatically by size, orientation, shape, when they occur in the heartbeat, and where they sit along the vessel. Nothing about it would help with stock prices.

Most of the work happens at level one. The slides called it the Pareto rule. You only go deeper when the simpler loop can’t find what you need, because each deeper level costs more computation and expertise, and is less general.

12. Sets and text

The short version. Sets are groups that can overlap, like “people who own a cat” and “people who own a dog”. The familiar circles of a Venn diagram stop working after three or four groups. Text is harder still, because words have meanings a computer doesn’t understand. Word clouds are the famous text visualization, and they have more weaknesses than strengths.

Sets

Set data shows up everywhere: which genres a film belongs to, which symptoms a patient has, which software a person uses. Bilal Alsallakh and colleagues surveyed the field and grouped the tasks into three levels [Alsallakh et al. 2014]. There are tasks about the sets themselves, like how big each set is and how much two sets overlap. There are tasks about the elements, like which sets a particular film belongs to. And there are tasks about the elements’ attributes, like whether films that are both comedy and drama get better ratings.

Venn and Euler diagrams. Both draw sets as closed curves, where overlap means intersection. A Venn diagram always draws every possible overlap, so three sets give eight regions, whether or not anything is in them. An Euler diagram only draws the overlaps that exist. If your sets are mammals, birds and cats, an Euler diagram puts cats inside mammals and keeps birds separate. A Venn diagram is forced to include a region for cats that are also birds, and an empty region still suggests “this could happen”.

Both break down quickly. A Venn diagram of six sets exists, and the course showed one shaped like bananas, but nobody can read it. Euler diagrams sometimes can’t be drawn with circles at all.

UpSet solves this by dropping the circles entirely [Lex et al. 2014]. It lists each combination of sets as a row in a matrix, with dots showing which sets are in the combination, and draws a bar chart of how many elements each combination has. It scales to many sets and lets you sort combinations by size. It takes a minute to learn and then it’s far more useful.

Text

Words are a strange kind of data. To a computer they are nominal, just labels. But there are tens of thousands of them, and they have meaning. “Norway”, “Bergen” and “Hardangervidda” are related. January comes before February. “Tennis”, “running” and “piano” are all hobbies. None of that is visible from the letters.

Kostiantyn Kucher and Andreas Kerren built a large visual survey of text visualization techniques, which is a good place to browse [Kucher and Kerren 2015]. The course grouped what people want from text into five tasks:

  • Understand a document by reading it, helped by highlighting.
  • Summarize it, to get the gist.
  • Cluster similar words or documents.
  • Quantify it, by turning words into numbers like frequency and rank.
  • Correlate text with other data. In “A Viz of Ice and Fire”, Fred Hohman and colleagues compared the dialogue in Game of Thrones scenes with each scene’s color palette [Hohman et al. 2017].

From words to numbers

Most text visualizations start with the same processing pipeline:

  1. Tokenize. Split the text into words. Decide what to do with numbers, hashtags and names like “Flesland Airport”.
  2. Remove stop words like “the”, “of” and “to”, which are frequent and meaningless.
  3. Stem or lemmatize. Group different forms of a word, so “visualization”, “visualize” and “visually” count as one, and “went” and “gone” become “go”.
  4. Count. The result is a “bag of words”, a list of words and how often they appear, with all order thrown away.
  5. Optionally, weight the counts. TF-IDF boosts words that are common in one document but rare across the collection, which is how you find what makes a document distinctive.

Word clouds

A word cloud, or tag cloud, draws the words from a bag of words with font size by frequency. The course was clear about the trade-offs.

Strengths: it’s good for getting the gist quickly, and for finding words to search for next.

Weaknesses:

  • Size is a weak channel compared to position.
  • Long words look bigger than short words with the same count.
  • The layout is random, so you can’t compare two clouds.
  • Raw frequency often isn’t meaningful. Without stop word removal, the biggest word is “the”.
  • All structure is lost. A bag of words has no order and no context.

A sorted bar chart of the top words is more accurate, if less pretty.

Keeping the context

Two techniques keep words in their context. A word tree picks a word or phrase and shows every continuation that follows it in the text, as a branching tree, like an interactive version of a concordance [Wattenberg and Viégas 2008]. Phrase nets draw a network of words connected by a chosen pattern, like “X and Y”, which reveals pairings in the text [van Ham, Wattenberg and Viégas 2009].

13. Fields and volumes: seeing inside things

The short version. Some data is a measurement at every point in space: the temperature across a country, or the density of tissue inside a body from a CT scan. You can show it three ways. Cut a slice through it. Pull out a surface where the value crosses a threshold, like the outline of a bone. Or render the whole volume as a semi-transparent cloud, deciding which values should glow and which should be see-through.

Fields

A field is continuous data, like temperature, measured at discrete points on a grid. If each point holds one number, it’s a scalar field. If it holds a direction and a strength, like wind, it’s a vector field.

Two terms matter here. Sampling is measuring a continuous thing at separate points. Interpolation is estimating the values between those points. The simplest kinds are nearest neighbor, which gives blocky results, and linear, which draws a straight line between samples. In 2D that becomes bilinear and in 3D trilinear. If you sample too sparsely, small features fall between the samples and no interpolation can bring them back.

Scalar fields come in every dimension:

  • 1D. Height along a hiking trail, drawn as a line plot.
  • 2D. Air temperature across a map, shown as a color map, or elevation shown with contour lines on a topographic map.
  • 3D. Tissue density inside a body from a CT or MRI scanner.

Voxels and cells

In 3D, data comes on a grid of voxels, short for volume elements, the way a pixel is a picture element. A voxel is a point sample in space, not a little cube. When you group the eight voxels at the corners of a small cube, you get a cell, and values inside the cell are found by trilinear interpolation. The slides called these two ways of interpreting the same data.

Three ways to show a scalar field

Slicing. Fix one coordinate and show the 2D slice. This is what a radiologist scrolls through on a CT viewer. Values are exact and nothing hides anything else, but the viewer has to assemble the 3D shape in their head.

Isocontours. Pick a value and show where the data equals it. In 2D these are isolines, like contour lines on a map. In 3D they’re isosurfaces, like the surface where tissue density equals that of bone. The classic algorithm is marching cubes [Lorensen and Cline 1987]. It visits each cell and checks which of its eight corners are above the threshold. That gives 256 cases, which reduce by symmetry to 15 basic ones, each with a ready-made pattern of triangles. It then interpolates along the cell edges to place the triangles where the surface actually crosses. The result is a clear surface, but only for one value. Everything is either inside or outside.

Direct volume rendering. Skip the surface entirely and render the volume itself, semi-transparently, so you can see several structures at once.

How a volume becomes an image

Direct volume rendering follows a pipeline described in Marc Levoy’s 1988 paper [Levoy 1988]. Picture a ray shot from your eye through each pixel of the screen and straight through the volume, taking samples at regular steps:

  1. Reconstruction. Estimate the value at each sample point from the surrounding voxels, usually by trilinear interpolation. Fancier filters exist. Stephen Marschner and Richard Lobb designed a test signal that shows how badly simple interpolation can do [Marschner and Lobb 1994].
  2. Classification. Decide what each value means and how it should look. A transfer function maps each value to a color and an opacity. Skin might be faint and transparent, bone white and solid. Designing that function is the hardest part. Joe Kniss and colleagues showed that also using how fast the value changes, the gradient, helps separate the boundaries between materials [Kniss, Kindlmann and Hansen 2002].
  3. Shading. Add lighting so surfaces look three-dimensional. Lighting models need to know which way a surface faces. In a volume, the gradient of the data points in that direction, so it acts as the surface normal.
  4. Compositing. Combine all the samples along the ray into one pixel color.

Unlike the ray tracing used in movies and games, the rays here don’t bounce or bend. Each sample simply adds some color and blocks some of the light from behind it.

Two details that matter

Classify before or after interpolating? If you apply the transfer function to each voxel first and then blend colors, thin features can vanish. Say only density 50 should glow red, and two neighboring voxels have densities 0 and 100. Both are transparent, so blending them gives transparent, and the membrane at 50 between them disappears. If you interpolate the densities first, the ray passes through 50 and catches it. Blending colors is not the same as coloring blended values. The second, post-interpolative approach is the modern standard.

Front to back or back to front? Compositing can start at the far end of the ray and paint each sample over the result, like layers of paint. Or it can start at the eye and add samples behind what’s already there, keeping track of how opaque the pixel has become. Both give the same image. Front to back has a big advantage: once the pixel is nearly opaque, everything behind it is invisible, so you can stop early. That trick is called early ray termination.

There are simpler ways to composite too. First hit shows the first sample above a threshold. Maximum intensity projection shows the brightest sample on the ray, which is popular for blood vessels filled with contrast agent. Averaging gives something like an X-ray.

14. Flow

The short version. Air flowing over a wing, blood through an artery, wind across a country. Flow data is a direction and a speed at every point. Engineers used to make it visible with smoke, dye and threads in wind tunnels. Today they simulate it on computers and draw lines and textures that mimic those old experiments.

Where flow data comes from

There are two sources. Experimental fluid dynamics makes real flow visible around a scale model, as with smoke in a wind tunnel. Computational fluid dynamics simulates the flow by solving the Navier-Stokes equations, then visualizes the result. The course’s point was that the computational methods were designed to mimic the experimental ones [Post and van Walsum 1993].

Physical experimentHow it worksComputer equivalent

Two ways to simulate

A simulation can watch the flow from fixed points or ride along with it. The Eulerian approach tracks velocity at fixed grid points as the fluid passes through, so quantities like density on the grid come for free. The Lagrangian approach tracks individual particles as they move, so their paths come for free, but properties of the fluid as a whole are harder to get.

Following the flow, step by step

To draw a line that follows the flow, you start at a point, look at the velocity there, take a small step, and repeat. This is numerical integration, and how you take each step matters a lot.

Euler integration takes a straight step in the direction of the arrow where you stand. On a curved flow it overshoots. In a flow going round in a circle it spirals outwards, as if there were a source in the middle that doesn’t exist. Its error shrinks in proportion to the step size.

Runge-Kutta 2 takes a half step first, measures the velocity at that midpoint, then goes back to the start and takes the full step using the midpoint velocity. That’s twice the work per step, and the error shrinks with the square of the step size.

Runge-Kutta 4 makes four trial measurements, at the start, twice at the midpoint and at the end, and takes one step with a weighted average. That’s four times the work, and the error shrinks with the fourth power of the step size. It’s the standard choice for complex flows.

Four kinds of lines

These are easy to mix up, and the difference only shows when the flow changes over time. Picture a chimney on a day when the wind keeps changing direction:

  • A streamline is drawn on a frozen snapshot of the flow. At one instant, start at a point and follow the arrows. It shows where the flow is heading right now. Think of a weather map’s wind lines at noon.
  • A pathline follows one particle over time. It is the actual route of a single puff of smoke, or a long-exposure photo of one glowing particle.
  • A streakline connects every particle that has passed through one fixed point. It is the visible smoke plume. When the wind turns, older smoke keeps drifting the old way, so the plume records the history of the wind at the chimney and isn’t the path of any single puff.
  • A timeline connects particles released at the same moment, like a row of hydrogen bubbles released along a wire, and shows how that line bends as it moves.

In steady flow that never changes, streamlines, pathlines and streaklines are identical.

Placing streamlines is its own problem. Too few and you miss details. Too many and it’s clutter. Bruno Jobard and Wilfrid Lefer’s method keeps lines evenly spaced. It starts new lines at a fixed distance from existing ones and stops a line when it gets too close to a neighbor [Jobard and Lefer 1997].

Points where the flow stops

Critical points are where the velocity is zero, and they organize the whole flow. At a source, flow spreads outwards. At a sink, it converges. At a saddle, it comes in along one direction and leaves along another. Around a center, it circles. Finding them gives a skeleton of the flow, often far simpler than the full picture.

Textures that show everything at once

Lines can miss what happens between them. Texture methods cover the whole picture instead:

  • Spot noise scatters thousands of small spots, each stretched along the flow, so the overlapping smears reveal the direction [van Wijk 1991].
  • Line integral convolution, or LIC, starts from random black-and-white noise. For each pixel it traces a short streamline and averages the noise along it, so pixels on the same streamline end up similar. The noise smears into streaks that follow the flow, just like oil on a wind tunnel model [Cabral and Leedom 1993].
  • Image-based flow visualization, or IBFV, warps an image along the flow every frame and blends in fresh noise. It produces smooth animations at interactive speeds, and you can inject virtual dye [van Wijk 2002]. There’s a demo on the author’s site.

The trade-off is that textures show the whole field but make individual paths, and which way along a streak the flow goes, less explicit.

15. Communicating with data

The short version. A chart you make to explore your own data and a chart you make to tell someone else something are different jobs. For communication, the chart has to be readable, it often helps if it’s memorable, and it usually works best inside a story. And every design choice nudges what people conclude, whether you mean it to or not.

Exploring versus presenting

Robert Kosara points out that most visualization research is about analysis, where you ask many questions of the data [Kosara 2016]. For that you want familiar, general charts that scale, like bar charts and scatterplots. Presentation is different. You want to get one point across and make it stick, and that justifies techniques an analyst would never use, like annotations, highlighting and a deliberate reading order.

Making a chart readable

A few concrete techniques from the course:

  • Banking to 45 degrees. People judge the slopes of lines most accurately when they’re around 45 degrees, so you can choose the chart’s aspect ratio to get there [Cleveland, McGill and McGill 1988]. Later work by Justin Talbot and colleagues showed it isn’t a hard rule [Talbot, Gerth and Hanrahan 2012].
  • Good tick marks. Talbot and colleagues formalized what makes axis labels good. Simplicity means round numbers like multiples of 10, 5 or 2. Coverage means labels reach close to the ends of the data. Density means not too many and not too few. Legibility means horizontal text with room to breathe [Talbot, Lin and Hanrahan 2010].
  • Log scale or a broken axis for data that spans many orders of magnitude. A log scale lets you compare everything directly but needs values above zero and a reader who understands it. A broken linear scale is more familiar but makes the reader do more work.
  • Data-ink ratio. Tufte argued for maximizing the share of ink that shows data, which means dropping heavy gridlines, borders and 3D effects [Tufte 1983].
  • Direct labels instead of legends. A legend makes the reader look back and forth and hold the mapping in working memory. Writing the name of each line at its end removes that work [Franconeri et al. 2021].

Chart junk, and when it isn’t junk

Tufte called decoration that doesn’t show data “chart junk” and wanted it gone. Research since then is more forgiving. Scott Bateman and colleagues found that embellished charts, like Nigel Holmes’ famous “Monstrous Costs” with its bars in a monster’s mouth, were read about as accurately as plain ones and remembered better [Bateman et al. 2010]. Patterns and textures, often dismissed as junk, help readers who can’t tell colors apart.

The rule I came away with: decoration that relates to the data helps memory and engagement, and decoration that doesn’t relate to it distracts. Simon Scarr’s “Iraq’s Bloody Toll” for the South China Morning Post turned a bar chart upside down and colored it blood red, so the bars drip down the page. Nothing is added. The encoding itself carries the emotion.

What makes a chart memorable

Michelle Borkin and colleagues showed people thousands of real visualizations and tested which ones they remembered [Borkin et al. 2013]. Memorable visualizations tended to:

  • include a recognizable object, like a person or an animal
  • be visually dense
  • have a low data-ink ratio, meaning plenty of “junk”
  • use many colors
  • use an unusual chart type

A follow-up study found people remember titles most, and that pictograms, used well, help recognition without hurting understanding [Borkin et al. 2016]. One slide in the course said it plainly: a memorable visualization is not necessarily a good visualization. Memorable matters most when you’re talking to the public, who see a chart once. An analyst looking at a dashboard every day needs precision.

Engagement and emotion

Engagement is a fuzzy word, and the course used a framework from Kevin Doherty and Gavin Doherty that splits it three ways [Doherty and Doherty 2018]. Cognitive engagement is thinking, the effort and attention someone puts in. Behavioral engagement is action, like clicking, scrolling and exploring. Emotional engagement is feeling.

Emotion is often measured on two scales. Valence is how pleasant something feels, and arousal is how intense. Helen Kennedy and Rosemary Lucy Hill interviewed ordinary people about the charts they meet in daily life and found that emotions shape how people engage with numbers, from pleasure in a beautiful chart to distrust of a source [Kennedy and Hill 2018]. A 2024 study found that even meaningless data with an upward trend makes people feel better than a downward one, and more variance makes them more tense [Blair, Wang and Perin 2024]. Designers can change that feeling with axis limits and scales, which is exactly why they should be careful.

Telling stories

Narrative visualization combines storytelling with data [Segel and Heer 2010]. Edward Segel and Jeffrey Heer studied dozens of examples from journalism and found seven genres: magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, and film or animation.

They also describe a spectrum. At one end, author-driven stories have a fixed order, lots of explanation and little interaction, like a documentary. At the other, reader-driven stories have no fixed order and lots of interaction, like a dashboard. Three common structures sit between them:

  • The martini glass. A narrow, author-driven stem tells the story first, then the glass opens up for free exploration.
  • The interactive slideshow. The author controls the order of slides, but readers can explore inside each one.
  • The drill-down story. The author sets a general theme, and readers choose which parts to open.

The lecture’s examples covered the spectrum. The New York Times piece “My Life With Long Covid” is author-driven. Die Zeit’s East-West exodus works like an interactive slideshow. The OECD’s Better Life Index is reader-driven, letting you weight what matters to you. Segel and Heer concluded that data stories tend to work best with constrained interaction at checkpoints.

Why stories at all? Science communicators used to assume a “deficit model”: give people enough information and they’ll know what to do. But people decide based on their goals, values and beliefs too. A story connects the data to those.

Framing

Every choice in a chart is an editorial choice: what data to include, how to encode it, what to put in the title, what to highlight. Jessica Hullman and Nicholas Diakopoulos call this visualization rhetoric [Hullman and Diakopoulos 2011]. The same data framed two ways leads people to different conclusions. You can’t avoid framing. You can be aware of it and honest about it.

16. Uncertainty

The short version. Almost every number comes with some doubt: measurement error, a forecast that could go several ways, missing data. Showing that doubt is more honest and helps people make better decisions. It’s also hard, because people misread uncertainty displays all the time, even experts.

What uncertainty is

The course’s working definition is a quantitative estimate of the error present in data. Two related ideas are worth separating:

  • Accuracy is how close a measurement is to the true value.
  • Precision is how close repeated measurements of the same thing are to each other.

They’re independent. Think of darts. All your darts in a tight cluster is precise. A cluster on the bullseye is precise and accurate. A tight cluster in the wrong corner is precisely wrong.

Two kinds of uncertainty

Epistemic uncertainty comes from a lack of knowledge, from the Greek word for knowledge. It covers bad measurements, missing data and poor models. You can reduce it by calibrating your instruments, collecting more data or building a better model.

Aleatoric uncertainty comes from randomness, from the Latin word for dice. It covers random measurement noise, natural variation, and the spread of a forecast. It is hard to reduce, because it’s built into the thing you’re measuring.

Why bother?

Franconeri and colleagues put it well: without understanding uncertainty, people can’t calibrate their sense of how reliable a pattern or claim is [Franconeri et al. 2021].

But there are honest reasons people leave it out. It can be hard to interpret. The chart may already be complex. And it can reduce trust, since some readers take visible uncertainty as a sign of weak analysis. Jessica Hullman interviewed authors and found that leaving uncertainty out is the norm. Many believe their chart shows a signal, their analysis has already validated it, and uncertainty would only blur it [Hullman 2020].

The challenges

The course listed five:

  1. It varies by domain. A 70% chance of rain is familiar. Uncertainty about where a tumor ends is a different matter.
  2. Visual channels are limited. If position and color already show the data, there’s little left for uncertainty.
  3. Uncertainty itself is complex, with many types and sources.
  4. The visualization adds uncertainty of its own, through binning, interpolation and color choices.
  5. People confuse uncertainty with certainty. A drawn boundary gets read as a hard line.

Ways to show it

The lecture listed strategies adapted from Kristin Potter and colleagues [Potter, Rosen and Johnson 2012]:

  • Show the distribution. A violin plot shows the full shape of the data, where a box plot can hide very different distributions behind identical boxes.
  • Show samples. Draw many possible outcomes. An ensemble of hurricane tracks, or hypothetical outcome plots that animate random draws one at a time.
  • Show a summary. A range like an error bar or a cone, or a visual property like blur, transparency or fading toward grey.

Error bars and the hurricane cone

Error bars are the most common display, and they confuse people. They can mean a standard deviation, a standard error or a confidence interval, which are very different things, and readers rarely know which. They also show the ends of a continuous distribution as if they were hard limits. The course showed a bar chart with two dots at the same distance from the mean, one just inside the bar and one just above it. People judged the one inside the bar more likely to belong. Visual boundaries become mental categories.

Hurricane forecasts show the same problem at a scale where it matters. The familiar “cone of uncertainty” shows where the center of the storm might go. Many people read it as the storm getting bigger. Le Liu and colleagues proposed drawing a small, well-chosen sample of possible tracks instead, so the spread is visible without a misleading outline [Liu et al. 2019].

Icon arrays

For communicating risk to the public, icon arrays work well. To show that a side effect affects 3 in 100 patients, draw 100 small person icons and color 3 of them. People understand this better than “3%”, and it beats written descriptions of probability [Franconeri et al. 2021]. Two arrays you want to compare need the same total, though. Comparing 3 out of 100 against 25 out of 1,000 defeats the purpose.

17. How do you know a visualization works?

The short version. A visualization can fail at four levels. You can misunderstand what people need. You can show the wrong data for their task. You can show the right data badly. Or your code can be too slow. Each level needs its own kind of check, and a fast, pretty chart doesn’t prove you solved the right problem.

Munzner’s nested model

Tamara Munzner’s nested model describes design as four levels, each inside the one before [Munzner 2009]:

  1. Domain. Who is this for, and what problem do they have?
  2. Abstraction. Translate their problem into general visualization terms. What data is shown, the data abstraction, and why they look at it, the task abstraction.
  3. Idiom. How is it shown and interacted with?
  4. Algorithm. How is it computed efficiently?

The nesting matters because mistakes flow inwards. If you misunderstood the problem, a perfect chart and fast code are still useless.

What can go wrong, and how to check

Each level has a typical threat, and checks you can make during development, called immediate validation, or afterwards, called downstream validation.

LevelThreatCheck during developmentCheck afterwards
DomainYou misunderstood their needsWatch and interview people at their real work, known as contextual inquirySee whether people actually adopt the tool
AbstractionYou’re showing them the wrong thingLet target users do their own work with a prototype, not tasks you made upTest with users from the target group, and study whether their behavior changes
IdiomThe way you show it doesn’t workJustify choices with perception research. Have experts review itLab studies that measure how well people do tasks
AlgorithmYour code is too slowAnalyze its complexityMeasure time and memory

Munzner also warns about mismatches, where you validate at the wrong level. Showing that your chart renders in 10 milliseconds says nothing about whether it shows the right data.

An honest look at my own project

Oslo on Two Wheels is an interactive data story my team built about Oslo’s city bikes, using open trip data from April 2019 to January 2026 matched with daily weather. Looking back at it with the nested model:

  • Domain. The story speaks to curious Oslo residents, commuters and leisure riders, and mentions the operators who move bikes around overnight. But we never wrote down one target audience and what they needed. That made every later decision harder to judge.
  • Abstraction. Because the audience was vague, so was the task. We had plenty of true, interesting views, like seasonal ridership, weather effects, and stations where far more bikes leave than arrive, but we struggled to settle on one clear story. In nested-model terms, that is a risk of showing the wrong thing. Not wrong data, just no clear answer to “what should a reader come away knowing?”
  • Idiom. We checked colors with accessibility tools and got feedback from classmates in peer review, which counts as expert review. One choice I’d change is the district map, which colors districts by the raw difference between departures and arrivals. Larger districts with more stations naturally get bigger numbers, which is the choropleth hazard from section 10. Normalizing per station or per trip would fix it.
  • Algorithm. We preprocessed millions of trips into small summary files and confirmed the site ran smoothly on an ordinary laptop.

Friends walked through the finished story while we noted where the transitions confused them. That’s a small downstream test. If I did it again, I’d start by writing down the audience and the one thing they should take away, and end by asking test readers what they remembered.

18. Accessibility

The short version. A chart that only works for people with perfect eyesight, a mouse and a steady hand excludes a lot of people. Accessibility means making the information available to everyone, through good contrast and labels, a data table, and sometimes sound or touch instead of sight.

Who it’s for

Frank Elavsky and colleagues define accessibility as “the practice of making information, content, and functionality fully available to and usable by people with disabilities” [Elavsky, Bennett and Moritz 2022]. The World Health Organization estimates that about 16% of the world’s population lives with some form of disability [WHO]. Disabilities can be visual, like color blindness or low vision. They can be cognitive, like dementia or a learning disability. Or they can be motor, like Parkinson’s disease or arthritis.

The course also talked about situational disability. Reading your phone in bright sunlight, listening to a message in a loud bar, or holding a child while trying to use a tablet. Accessible design helps all of these. A line from the slides I like: accessibility is not a feature, it is necessary for a user’s experience to exist at all.

Guidelines

The general web standard is the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, WCAG. It is built on four principles. Content must be perceivable, operable, understandable and robust, often shortened to POUR.

For charts specifically, Elavsky and colleagues made Chartability, a set of about 50 testable heuristics organized under POUR plus three more principles: compromising, assistive and flexible [Elavsky, Bennett and Moritz 2022]. Some of the critical ones:

  • Low contrast. Marks need enough contrast against the background, and text more.
  • Content is only visual. If the data only exists in the picture, a screen reader user gets nothing.
  • No title, summary or caption. Without them, nobody knows the main point.
  • No table. A data table lets a screen reader user go through every value.
  • Seizure risk. Avoid flashing more than three times a second.
  • Only one way to interact. Mouse-only charts exclude keyboard users.

Beyond sight: sound

Sonification maps data to sound. The main channels are pitch, volume, tempo and timbre, the quality that makes a piano sound different from a violin. Timbre works like hue, good for categories rather than amounts. LoudNumbers’ sonification of the UK’s July 2022 heatwave was one of the lecture’s examples.

Hearing works differently from seeing. Vision takes in a whole chart at once. Sound arrives one piece at a time, so it’s serial and leans on memory. Sonification is faster than having a screen reader read each number aloud, but it isn’t always accurate, and mappings aren’t always intuitive. What should “louder” mean for an ordered category?

Beyond sight: touch

Tactile graphics, like raised-line charts or 3D-printed models, let blind and visually impaired people explore data spatially, which speech can’t easily give them. They’re common in education and outreach, and unlike sound, touch lets you explore several parts of a chart whenever you want, in any order.

Touch has its own limits, though. There’s no preattentive pop-out, so heavy gridlines, crowded elements and ambiguous textures get confusing fast. Reading takes hand movement, and finding your orientation on the chart is critical. Tactile graphics can also be costly to produce, in both time and money. Newer devices like refreshable braille pads and haptic feedback are starting to change that.

19. What I took away

If I had to compress the whole course into a few sentences, these would be them.

A visualization is a tool for a task, and it’s judged by whether it helps someone do that task better. Pretty is optional.

Most of the craft comes down to marks and channels. Put the most important thing on the most accurate channel, usually position. Let perception do the work, so the reader doesn’t have to think.

Every step, from cleaning data to choosing a color scale, is a decision that shapes what people see. My own sunshine map showed me that you can mislead without meaning to.

Honesty isn’t one rule. Starting the axis at zero can hide the truth as easily as cutting it off. The real question is always what change matters, to whom.

And the human stays in the loop. The best tools, from a radiologist’s volume renderer to an engineer’s linked views of a simulation, don’t replace judgment. They give it something to work with.

Alberto Cairo, quoted in the accessibility lecture, said that a picture is worth a thousand words, if you know how to read it. A lot of this field is about making sure more people can.
